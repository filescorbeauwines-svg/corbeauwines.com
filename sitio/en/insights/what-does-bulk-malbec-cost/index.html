What does bulk Malbec cost? It depends which Malbec

Three buyers can ask for exactly the same thing and need three different wines. This piece is about working out which one is yours before comparing a figure.

Published 11 September 2026

An enquiry lands in the inbox. Variety, volume, destination. "We're looking for bulk Malbec — what price can you offer?"

Behind those few words there might be a supermarket brand fighting for a shelf price, a specialist-retail proposition that needs to pull away from entry level, or a wine that has to hold its own on a restaurant list.

All three are asking for Malbec. All three are buying it in bulk.

None of them needs the same wine.

Asking the price first is reasonable

Worth saying before going further, because that question tends to get treated as a buyer's mistake. It is not.

Whoever writes that email is usually approaching several suppliers at once and needs to rule most of them out quickly. Price is the cheapest filter available: one figure, one decision. Perfectly rational behaviour.

The problem is not asking. The problem is that this filter, used alone, lets exactly the wrong projects through: it eliminates the supplier who quoted with everything included and keeps the one who left half the costs out.

What is needed is a better filter, not less filtering. And the better filter fits in one paragraph of the same email.

That paragraph is at the end of this piece. First, it helps to understand what is being compared.

The numbers show where bulk sits, not what is in it

According to the OIV, in 2025 bulk wine — containers over ten litres, in that statistic — accounted for 34% of global export volume and 7.3% of its value.

FuenteOIV, State of the World Wine Sector in 2025, page 18

That gap between volume and value invites a conclusion the data does not support, so it is worth reading carefully. These are two distinct export categories, covering different products, origins, quality levels and presentations. The comparison measures neither how the value of a single wine is distributed nor at which stage it is generated.

What it does describe is the nature of the format. Bulk is exported without its final presentation, and that presentation gets completed at destination. Which is why its average price is not comparable with bottled wine, and why it is no use for quoting: it pools countries, varieties, quality levels and transactions with nothing in common.

The useful reading for a buyer is a different one: bulk lets you develop part of the product's value at destination, on a quality that already has to exist at origin.

The Argentine data behaves similarly. The INV brings the format into focus: in the first half of 2026, within single-varietal red Malbec, 196,575 of the 550,819.5 hectolitres exported went in bulk.

That is 19.66 million litres: 35.7% of the volume exported in that category. The 2026 figures are provisional.

FuenteINV, Exportaciones de vinos varietales, first half 2026, page 4

But that statistic measures how much Malbec is sold in bulk, not how much of it is entry, entry plus, premium or high end. The format is identified; the commercial tier is not.

The statistics not breaking it out does not mean the segmentation is absent. Ciatti's June 2025 report separated three categories of Argentine bulk Malbec — Standard, Premium and High End — with distinct references under a single commercial condition. The segmentation is documented; what does not exist is a public source stating how much volume sits in each tier.

FuenteCiatti, Global Market Report, June 2025, page 6

And there is a sensory reason behind it. A 2024 study in npj Science of Food analysed 81 Malbec wines from 29 plots across three vintages and found differences linked to origin, with a strong vintage effect. Sharing a variety does not mean sharing aroma, flavour or mouthfeel.

FuenteUrvieta and colleagues, Tracing the origin of Argentine Malbec wines by sensometrics

Which is why asking for "Malbec from Mendoza" narrows part of the offering and leaves everything else open.

Quality and style are two questions, not one

They get mixed up constantly, and separating them saves whole conversations.

The first: what quality level the project needs. How much definition, balance, complexity and length the wine has to deliver to hold its position.

The second: what style. Fresher or riper fruit, softer or firmer texture, more or less oak, immediate expression or slower development in the glass.

Two wines can be equally well made and offer opposite styles. The buyer picks one because of how it fits their consumer, not because the other is worse.

To organise the assessment, six points worth agreeing up front.

DimensionWhat to agree
Fruit and aromatic expressionWhich character is wanted and how prominent it should be.
Texture and structureWhat mouthfeel the wine has to deliver.
BalanceHow freshness, alcohol, tannin and any residual sugar integrate.
OakHow much presence, and what job it does within the style.
OriginWhich provenance the project needs and how it gets documented.
ContinuityWhich attributes have to hold across subsequent lots.

A working proposal between buyer and supplier, not an official quality classification.

Three buyers, three decisions

Three projects, by way of example. Not real Corbeau transactions.

A supermarket brand on a tight price

Prioritises clear fruit expression, accessible texture and continuity of supply. Here a more expensive alternative has to justify itself with an improvement the consumer actually notices.

A brand for specialist retail

Wants to pull away from the entry-level reference. Likely to prioritise profile definition, length, and a provenance it can communicate with backing. The work lies in identifying which attributes justify the next rung, and how they show up in the glass.

A wine for on-trade

Points to a Malbec that works alongside a specific food offering, with a given freshness and texture. Selection answers that use, as well as the target cost and supply terms.

None of those channels defines a quality level on its own. What the three examples show is how the decision changes once the request carries a commercial objective inside it.

What justifies an additional cost

Improving a wine is the easy half of the conversation. The hard half, and the more useful one, is knowing when that improvement returns what it costs.

There are no universal rules here. Oak, structure or a particular provenance may deliver exactly the profile a project needs, or may add nothing at all: it depends on the product. What can be done is to ask the right question before requesting the change.

  • Does it improve the profile this project needs?. Not whether it improves wine in the abstract. Whether it improves this wine, for this consumer and this use.
  • Does the buyer perceive that improvement?. It may be in the glass without being on the label. A provenance, for instance, can deliver the profile sought without needing to be communicated.
  • Does it fit the project's economics?. A wine can be better and still be unviable. If the improvement does not fit the budget, it is not an improvement: it is a different problem.
  • Can it be sustained across successive vintages and batches?. An attribute that shows up in the sample and not in the reorder creates more problems than it solves.

The question is not whether an attribute is good. It is whether it adds something to this product, and whether that contribution justifies what it costs.

When all four answers are positive, there is a firmer basis for justifying the additional cost. When one is negative, better to say so before the sample than after the container.

What a twenty-cent difference means

The quality discussion has to come back to numbers, even when they are not quotation numbers.

Take two alternatives twenty US cents a litre apart, everything else equal. For a 750 ml bottle, the direct difference in wine cost is:

US$0.20 × 0.75 = US$0.15 per bottle

Across a 100,000-bottle programme — 75,000 litres before losses — that is USD 15,000.

US$0.20US$0.150US$15,000
US$0.30US$0.225US$22,500

Illustrative arithmetic on a hypothetical difference. These are not prices or quotations: only the effect of a cost delta per litre across a full programme, excluding losses, financing, duties and any change in other expenses.

One clarification, because the question always comes up. Every quotation is prepared against volume, profile, schedule and delivery terms. A figure taken outside that context does not inform: it misleads.

Fifteen cents a bottle is a reasonable investment if it gets you the profile the brand needs. On a tight-margin project it can be impossible. It depends on the product and its economics.

The useful question is what you get in return: a specific sensory improvement, a required origin, a particular specification, or another condition of value you can verify.

And one more caution: that increase is not the same as the change needed in the consumer price. Other costs, duties and margins sit in between.

Two quotations are only comparable when they include the same things

Price per litre creates a false sense of precision.

To compare offers, both have to state volume, currency, price validity, place and terms of delivery, the wine's state of preparation, and which tasks remain before bottling.

The International Chamber of Commerce recommends spelling out the costs that typically create discrepancies — loading, unloading, terminal handling and other charges — even once an Incoterm has been chosen.

FuenteICC, Incoterms 2020 Checklist and Flowcharts, page 4

For wine you have to add an operational definition: which analyses accompany the lot, which specifications it has to meet, and who carries out any additional preparation agreed.

A cheaper offer may be leaving costs on the buyer's side. A dearer one may already include work the other still needs.

Until both sit on the same basis, the price difference says nothing about which is better value. That is the trap in the quick filter from the start.

The second order starts with the first

A single lot solves an immediate need. A brand expecting repeat sales needs more than that.

Before closing the first order, three things are worth knowing: what volume stands behind the sample, how long it can be held, and how things will work once the lot or the vintage changes.

Holding continuity does not mean denying that wine varies. It means defining which features carry the product's identity and how future alternatives will be approved.

It also helps to separate two things people conflate: buying an available opportunity is not the same as developing a reference to sustain over time. Both can be right, but they ask for different commitments.

The paragraph that replaces the price filter

Back to the email at the start. With these five points, that enquiry stops needing a figure to be useful.

  • Destination market and channel. Where it sells and through what, because labelling and much of the profile follow from there.
  • Target shelf price. Not the price of the wine: the price of the bottle on the shelf. Everything works backwards from there.
  • Expected volume and schedule. For the first order and for the year, if there is a horizon.
  • Profile sought. A physical sample, or a description of what should be kept and what should change from a known reference.
  • Delivery terms. Place, Incoterm, and which tasks you expect to be included.

Put into a single paragraph, the email from the start reads like this:

Enquiry template

We are looking for a Malbec for [market and channel], with a target shelf price of [amount], an initial volume of [litres] and deliveries scheduled for [dates]. We want a [description] profile, using [sample or known label] as the reference. We need a quotation under [Incoterm and place], with a breakdown of the tasks included.

With that, a tasting stops being a formality and becomes a concrete discussion: what each alternative brings, what that difference costs, and which one answers the project better.

From the origin

At Corbeau we work across different levels and styles of bulk Malbec, and the conversation always starts in the same place: who the client wants to sell to, and in which market. The alternatives we put forward come from that, and so do the adjustments.

Price remains an essential question. It is just that the answer is only worth something once it is clear which wine is being bought, for which product, and with what chance of sustaining it over time.
