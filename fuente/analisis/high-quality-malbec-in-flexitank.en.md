There is a question that surfaces whenever a buyer selects a higher-quality wine for a bulk programme. Sometimes they ask it. More often they just think it.

> Can I ship this in bulk without losing what I am paying more for?

Behind that doubt sits a widespread association: flexitank is the cheap format, and cheap degrades. It is a reasonable belief, and there is evidence that lets us examine it.

## What was studied

The Australian Wine Research Institute examined bulk shipments between Australia and the UK involving more than a million litres across fifteen months, with chemical, sensory and temperature assessments taken in transit.

The central comparison set two transport systems against each other: ISO tanks and flexitanks.

> A panel of experienced winemakers found no consistent sensory preference for either one.

FUENTE [AWRI, Transport and storage](https://www.awri.com.au/industry_support/winemaking_resources/storage-and-packaging/post-packaging/transport-and-storage/)

The conclusion is precise and worth taking exactly as stated: in those trials, neither system was consistently preferred by the panel.

## What the study does not cover

Three clarifications, because data pushed too far stops being useful.

- **It did not compare bulk against bottled.** That is a separate argument and needs separate evidence.
- **It did not assess Malbec specifically.** The route and varieties in the trial were different.
- **It does not say transport is irrelevant.** This result compares preference between systems. On its own it does not demonstrate that the wine underwent no changes in transit.

Even within those limits, the finding covers what matters for the decision: in those trials there was no sensory preference favouring the ISO tank over the flexitank.

## The commercial consequence

Since the panel showed no consistent preference between the two systems, the decision on a higher-value programme should not be made by looking at format alone.

> A Malbec should not be ruled out of a higher-value project simply because it is supplied in bulk.

What does bear on the outcome is the whole operation: how it is loaded, how it is protected, how long it spends in transit, at what temperatures, and with what controls the wine's condition gets documented at each stage.

The OIV good practices guide identifies oxidation and contamination as the two risks to control, and sets out sampling procedures to record the wine's condition during the journey.

FUENTE [OIV, Good Practices Guide for Bulk Wine Transportation, sections 3 and 5.2](https://www.oiv.int/node/3830/download/pdf)

## Three checks for a specific purchase

In practice, this comes down to three moments where a record has to exist.

- **Before loading.** Confirm the lot matches the approved sample, and record its analytical and sensory characteristics. Without that initial reference there is nothing to compare against later.
- **In transit.** Agree handling, protection and transport conditions, with clear responsibility for each leg.
- **On arrival, before bottling.** Check the wine against retained samples and agreed specifications. Catching a deviation here allows a decision before bottling proceeds, though not every deviation can be put right.

Those three checks do more than control quality: they make the selection verifiable. The buyer stops trusting that the wine arrived well and becomes able to demonstrate it.

NOTA Paying more for a better selection only makes sense if that selection arrives intact. What the research lets you decide is narrow and useful: format alone is not reason enough to rule out a higher-quality wine. What is worth examining is the operation, and that depends on how the work is done.
